PRE-MATCH

Tennis is one of the few sports played seriously on three completely different surfaces, each rewarding a different style of play so strongly that entire careers are built around one of them.

This page covers the game's odd scoring system, where the sport came from, what changes physically between grass, clay, and hard courts, and how far technology has already reached into a sport built on tradition. Nothing here is for sale — it's simply an account of the game.

THE SCORING

Zero is called "love," and nobody is fully sure why

Tennis scores within a game in an order that matches no other sport: 0, 15, 30, 40, game — not 1, 2, 3, 4. Several theories exist for why, and none is proven. The most repeated explanation ties the point values to a clock face, with the score climbing in quarter-hours toward 60; another points to the French "l'oeuf" (the egg), a zero-shaped visual pun that may have become "love" in English. Historians simply don't have a confirmed answer.

Love

Zero points. Possibly from the egg-shaped zero ("l'oeuf" in French), possibly unrelated — the origin isn't settled.

15

The first point won in a game.

30

The second point — following the same quarter-hour logic as 15.

40

The third point. It should be "45" to keep the pattern, but was shortened at some point in the sport's history for reasons that, again, aren't fully documented.

Deuce

A 40–40 tie. A player must then win two points in a row — advantage, then game — to close it out.

ORIGINS

From a French monastery courtyard to a London garden party

Tennis traces back to "jeu de paume" ("game of the palm"), played in French monastery courtyards as early as the 12th century, where players struck a ball with a bare or gloved hand rather than a racket. Many of the sport's oddities come from this period — including the call of "tenez" ("take this" or "here it comes"), which historians believe is the likely root of the word "tennis" itself.

The modern outdoor game — "lawn tennis" — was patented in 1874 by British army officer Walter Wingfield, who marketed a box set with a net, rackets, and rules intended for garden parties. The All England Club, previously devoted to croquet, held the first Wimbledon Championships in 1877, and the sport spread quickly through the same country-club culture that croquet had occupied.

THE SEASON

One sport, three surfaces, four majors

The tennis calendar runs through four Grand Slam tournaments, each on a different surface, and top players have to adjust their entire game as the year moves from one to the next.

Australian Open

January · Hard court · Melbourne
  • Opens the season on Melbourne's Rebound Ace and Plexicushion courts, played in some of the hottest conditions of any major.
  • A true medium-pace surface — favors all-around players over pure specialists.

Roland Garros

May–June · Red clay · Paris
  • The ball sits up higher and slower off clay than any other surface, stretching rallies and rewarding heavy topspin.
  • The only major that still relies on umpires checking the physical ball mark left in the clay rather than full electronic line calling.

Wimbledon

June–July · Grass · London
  • The fastest, lowest-bouncing surface, historically the home of serve-and-volley tennis.
  • From 2025, Wimbledon retired its human line judges after 147 years in favor of full electronic line calling.

US Open

August–September · Hard court · New York
  • Closes the season on a fast hard court, often at night under lights, in front of the loudest crowds on tour.
  • Was among the first majors to adopt electronic line calling on show courts, well ahead of Wimbledon.
THE SERVE

The fastest moving object in mainstream sport

A tennis serve is, by most measures, the fastest a ball moves in any widely played sport — faster than a baseball pitch, a cricket delivery, or a hockey slap shot. The outright record belongs to Australia's Sam Groth, clocked at 263.4 km/h (163.7 mph) in 2012, though that mark came at a lower-tier event; the fastest serve at a top-level ATP match belongs to John Isner, at 253 km/h (157.2 mph) during the 2016 Davis Cup.

Spin does as much work as speed. A "kick" serve uses heavy topspin to make the ball leap unpredictably off the bounce, while a slice serve curves sideways through the air, pulling a returner wide off the court. Both rely on precise racket-face angle at contact — a difference measured in single degrees separates a clean ace from a fault.

263 km/h

The outright fastest serve ever recorded, by Sam Groth in 2012.

253 km/h

The fastest serve recognized at top-tier ATP level, by John Isner in 2016.

11h 5m

THE LINE CALL

The slow removal of the human eye

Electronic line calling has spread through tennis gradually rather than all at once, and the order in which it arrived says something about each event's relationship to tradition.

2006
The US Open and Wimbledon introduce Hawk-Eye as a limited player challenge system, used only when a call is disputed.
2021
The Australian Open becomes the first Grand Slam to remove line judges entirely, using full electronic calling on every court.
2022
The US Open follows, ending the on-court role of human line judges.
2025
Wimbledon retires all 300 of its line judges after 147 years, adopting full electronic line calling. The French Open, played on clay where a ball's mark is still physically visible, remains the only major still using human line judges.
MATCH POINT

A sport still arguing with its own traditions

Tennis is unusual in how visibly it wrestles with change: a scoring system nobody can fully explain, a Grand Slam calendar that still asks its best players to master three different surfaces in a single season, and a decades-long, court-by-court transition from human line judges to cameras. Whatever comes next, it will likely arrive the way electronic line calling did — one major at a time, with the French Open holding out longest.
